Start here

If you read one page, read this one. The short version is the top third; everything below it is detail you can come back for.

The short version

  • It's the real webCoRE editor. Not a lookalike. Your pistons are stock webCoRE JSON, and the editor is the same one you already know.
  • There is no webCoRE engine. Pistons are compiled into native Home Assistant automations. Nothing interprets them at runtime.
  • Compiling is not running. A piston can save and compile perfectly and still not do what you meant. Test it.
  • Your automations survive PistonCore. Delete it tomorrow and the automations it wrote keep working — they're ordinary HA files.
  • Commands come in two kinds. Plain ones (Turn on) and ones tagged custom (light.turn_on). Prefer the plain ones. Why →
  • Some pistons need PyScript. Simple ones don't. Formulas, loops and variables do. Nothing breaks until you use one.
  • It's alpha. Don't put anything safety-critical on it yet.

That's the whole picture. The rest of this page explains the parts that surprise people.

How this differs from webCoRE on a hub

On Hubitat / SmartThingsHere
webCoRE runs your piston, step by step, forever Your piston is translated once into an HA automation. Home Assistant runs that. PistonCore isn't in the loop.
Edit a piston, it takes effect immediately Same — saving recompiles and deploys. But it must compile first, and a failure is shown rather than ignored.
Every command your device driver offers is available Same idea, from Home Assistant instead: services your devices actually support are offered, tagged custom.
If webCoRE stops, your automations stop If PistonCore stops, your automations keep running.

The two kinds of command — and which to pick

When you add a task you'll see commands tagged three ways:

TagWhat it is
device webCoRE's own command, e.g. Turn on. Prefer these.
emulated webCoRE builds it from simpler ones, e.g. Toggle. Also fine.
custom A Home Assistant service offered directly, e.g. light.turn_on. Use when nothing above does what you need.

Why prefer the plain ones

They're more portable, and that's the real reason. Turn on means "turn this thing on" whatever it turns out to be — swap a bulb for a smart switch and the piston still works. light.turn_on is pinned to a light, and stops making sense the moment the device isn't one.

They also read better, they carry proper descriptions, and they're the ones that survive Home Assistant renaming things underneath you.

Why the custom ones exist anyway

Because webCoRE's vocabulary was finished years ago, and Home Assistant keeps growing. Lawn mowers, voice assistants, to-do lists, media grouping — webCoRE has no words for any of it, and never will. The custom commands are how you reach them.

They're also an escape hatch. If a plain command ever does the wrong thing for your device, the raw service is right there and you can work around it yourself instead of waiting for someone to fix it.

Only services your device actually supports are offered — Home Assistant is asked what each device can do, so you shouldn't see options that would just fail.

What PistonCore writes to Home Assistant

Four things, no fifth: its own compiled automations, two include lines in configuration.yaml (shown to you before it touches anything), a location-mode helper, and the test-devices integration if you ask for it. All listed with removal instructions in the install guide.

When something doesn't work

  • The piston list shows a status on every row — that's the first place a problem appears.
  • The piston's own page shows the error in full.
  • Settings → Diagnostics checks the whole instance and can hand you a ready-made repair prompt to give an AI.

Errors are never silent, and never hidden in a log you'd have to know about.
